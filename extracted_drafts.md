What to Expect from a Thorough Assessment

[QUICK ANSWER — top of page] A thorough assessment has four parts: a detailed history, an examination of how you move — where range is lost and where your left and right sides no longer match, testing of the individual joints and spinal segments, and evaluation of your muscles as a working system. Asymmetry in movement is a key indicator as is an abnormal range of motion. The findings, not assumptions, guide the treatment.

How do you know whether a therapist will really assess you?

I can only tell you what I do and why. My assessment-based treatments begins with your pain but also the question of *why* it hurts.

This page describes what each of those steps involves and what they are designed to find, so you know what to expect — and what to ask — wherever you seek care. The title alone doesn't tell you how a therapist works.

There are practical signs you can look for, whoever you're considering. Does the first visit begin with a structured history and physical examination before any hands-on treatment? Are you asked to bring clothing that allows your spine and joints to be observed? Are specific tests performed — and are the findings explained to you in plain language before treatment starts? Does the treatment plan follow from those findings?

This page describes what each of those steps involves and what they are designed to find, so you know what to expect — and what to ask — wherever you seek care.

Step 1: What happens during the clinical history?

Your first visit begins with a conversation, not a table. Eva takes a detailed history: when the symptoms started, what movements or situations trigger them, what makes them better or worse, how they affect your daily function, and what has already been tried. Previous imaging (X-ray, MRI) and medical records add useful context if you have them.

The history does two jobs. It narrows down the likely mechanical sources of the problem before the physical examination begins, and it establishes your goals — because treatment for a trail runner with recurring hip pain is planned differently from treatment for someone who can't sit through a workday.

Many patients arrive having tried other approaches without lasting results. A careful history often surfaces patterns — how the pain returns, what it responds to — that often point toward factors not previously identified.

Step 2: What does the physical examination involve?

After the history, Eva examines how you actually move, in detail. The body is built to move symmetrically and efficiently, and it works as one connected system — joints, muscles, and the movement patterns that coordinate them. When one side moves less than the other, when a segment is restricted, or when a muscle group is doing work its counterpart should share, the body compensates. Those compensations — asymmetries and departures from efficient movement — are the key indicators the examination looks for, because they point to where the problem is being driven from, not just where it is felt. This functional, whole-system view — treating the movement pattern rather than only the painful spot — is the tradition of Czech physicians Karel Lewit and Vladimir Janda (with whom I have completed several courses).

The examination reads three connected layers together — movement, joints, and muscles. No single layer gives the answer on its own; the picture comes from how the findings line up.

How is movement tested?

[VIDEO 1 — movement testing]

Movement testing looks at how you move as a whole. Eva observes and measures your active movement — bending, turning, reaching — and reads it against two references: the range that movement should have, and the symmetry between your left and right sides.

Asymmetry is the key indicator here. A movement that is restricted or uneven side-to-side shows where the body has adapted or compensated, often well away from the spot that hurts. And a movement that reproduces your familiar pain shows which structures are actually involved — a controlled way of letting the pain point to its own source.

This is why movement testing comes first. It tells Eva where to look more closely with the joint and muscle testing that follows.

How are joints tested? The spine as an example.

[VIDEO 2 — segmental joint testing]

The spine is not one joint but a chain of segments, and each segment contributes its own share of movement. Eva tests the segments individually — how each one moves, and whether it moves the same to the left as to the right.

A segment that moves freely one way but is restricted the other is an imbalance, and it is treatable, because the restriction sits in the tissues around the segment: the joint itself, the ligaments, and the muscles that control it. Karel Lewit described how these local restrictions link into chain reactions through the locomotor system — one reason a restricted segment can show up far from where it is felt. This is detail that whole-body movement testing can't reach. Two people with the same back pain can have restrictions at completely different segments — which is why the same treatment can help one and do little for the other.

How are muscles tested?

[VIDEO 3 — muscle system evaluation]

Muscles are tested as a system, alongside the movement and joint findings — not as isolated parts. Eva assesses tension, tightness, and the balance between muscle groups: which muscles are overworking, which are underactive, and how those patterns fit the restrictions found in the earlier tests. The tendency for certain muscles to tighten while their opposites weaken — in predictable patterns — was mapped by neurologist Vladimir Janda.

This matters because the muscle findings direct the treatment. A muscle held in chronic tension needs release or stretching; its underactive counterpart needs activation and training. Treating one without the other tends to give short-lived results, because the imbalance driving the tension is still there.

Read together, the three layers complete the picture: where the problem shows up, which segments are involved, and which muscular imbalances are holding it in place.

Step 3: How does treatment begin?

Treatment follows the findings — and it's often gradual. Once the assessment is complete, Eva explains what was found in plain language, then begins treatment in the same visit where time allows. Techniques are chosen to match the findings: massage and soft-tissue work including fascia, joint mobilization for restricted segments, specific stretching, and neuromuscular techniques to restore normal movement control.

Because the body adapts progressively, treatment is planned the same way. Some acute problems resolve in two to three sessions. Chronic imbalances that have built up over years are typically corrected step by step, with each session building on the changes from the last. Mild soreness for 24–48 hours after treatment is common as the body adjusts.

Eva will discuss a realistic plan of care with you based on the findings and your goals — including, where relevant, periodic maintenance.

Step 4: What about training and preventing it from coming back?

Lasting results depend on what happens between sessions. As treatment restores normal movement and balance, Eva adds training and instructions to keep it: home exercises matched to your specific findings, with instruction on how to perform them correctly, and practical guidance on the positions and habits that fed the problem in the first place.

For many patients this includes elements of Dynamic Neuromuscular Stabilization (DNS) — an active approach to training the deep stabilizing system of the trunk: the diaphragm, pelvic floor, and abdominal wall working together. DNS was developed from the developmental-kinesiology work of Pavel Kolář and colleagues at the Prague School of Rehabilitation (Frank C, Kobesová A, Kolář P. Dynamic neuromuscular stabilization & sports rehabilitation. International Journal of Sports Physical Therapy. 2013;8(1):62–73). The aim throughout is self-management: you leave not only with less pain, but with an understanding of what was driving it and the tools to keep it from returning.

What should you bring, and how long is the first visit?

Book 60 minutes for your initial visit if you can so there is time for both the full assessment and treatment (45 minutes is also workable). Please arrive 10 minutes early to complete the intake form covering your medical history and current symptoms.

Bring a tank top and shorts — the examination depends on being able to observe your spine, posture, and joint movement directly, while keeping you comfortable. If you have previous imaging (X-rays, MRI) or diagnostic reports, bring them; they're not required, but they add valuable context.

Why Does Pain Keep Coming Back After Treatment?

[QUICK ANSWER — top of page] When pain keeps returning, it usually means the root cause was never identified — treatment eased the symptom where it hurt without addressing why it started. The reasons differ from person to person: often a movement or stabilisation pattern the body has adapted around, sometimes a nervous system that has grown more sensitive to pain over time. Finding which applies to you takes a thorough assessment.

You have probably tried a number of things already. Maybe you had some relief — but then, weeks or months later, the same pattern in your lower back, neck, shoulder, or hip shows up again. More often it means the underlying cause hasn't been found and addressed yet.

Why does the same pain keep coming back?

The most common reason is the treatment did not adequately address why it hurt. Easing a tight muscle or a painful joint can bring real relief — but if the pattern that overloaded that area in the first place is still there, the pain comes back for a reason.

This is not unusual. In a large study following people with new low back pain, only about half recovered cleanly; roughly a third relapsed repeatedly over the following two years (Norton et al., Spine Journal, 2015). Recurring pain is one of the most common patterns in musculoskeletal care.

What separates lasting relief from the cycle of return is whether the actual cause was found. That is the job of a thorough assessment — and the reasons pain recurs are varied enough that they can only be identified by examining how you move and stabilise, not by assuming. (See What to Expect from a Thorough Assessment.)

Two people, identical pain — completely different causes

Two people can walk in with the same lower back pain and have entirely different reasons for it. One may have a stabilisation pattern that overloads the spine. Another may have a restricted joint segment feeding the problem from elsewhere. A third may have a long-standing habit — how they sit, lift, or breathe — quietly driving it.

The pain looks the same. The cause is not. This is why treatment aimed only at the painful spot tends to give temporary relief: it can calm the symptom, but it doesn't reach whichever driver is unique to that person. Without examining how your body moves and stabilises, the same overload keeps happening — and the pain keeps coming back.

I've tried physiotherapy, chiropractic, or massage — why does it still come back?

This is one of the most common frustrations patients describe: months of treatment, some relief each time, and then the same problem returns.

Physiotherapy, chiropractic, and massage therapy are all legitimate, useful tools, and each can help. What determines whether relief lasts is less about which profession you saw and more about whether the underlying cause was found and addressed. Treatment that targets only the painful area — however skilled — leaves the driver in place if that driver sits elsewhere in how you move and stabilise.

Sciatica is a clear example. Nerve-related leg pain is often driven by patterns in the lower back, pelvis, and hips rather than only at the spot where it's felt. If treatment concentrates on the leg or the local symptom without assessing what is loading the nerve upstream, it's common for the pain to ease and then return. The question that changes the outcome is not "what type of therapist," but "was the actual cause assessed?"

<a name="assessment-finds"></a>What a thorough assessment finds that surface treatment misses

Two of the most commonly overlooked are worth explaining, because they help make sense of why pain persists.

A stabilisation pattern the body no longer performs well. In people with recurrent low back pain, the deep stabilising muscle of the trunk (transversus abdominis) has been shown to activate late, and the movement's control in the brain's motor map reorganises — the nervous system keeps defaulting to an old, compensatory pattern. Importantly, the same research found this is reversible: targeted retraining shifted the motor map back toward normal and restored the muscle's timing (Tsao, Galea & Hodges, European Journal of Pain, 2010).

A nervous system that has become more sensitive. When pain persists, the nervous system itself can become more reactive, so that less is needed to trigger it — a well-documented process called central sensitization (Woolf, Pain, 2011). This does not mean the pain is imagined; it is a real, physical change in how the system processes signals, and it helps explain why pain can outlast the original injury.

Neither of these is universal, and neither can be assumed — which is the point. They are found, or ruled out, through the structured history, movement, joint, and muscle testing described on the Thorough Assessment page. The assessment is what turns "the pain keeps coming back" into a specific, addressable finding.

What actually helps: retraining the pattern

Once the assessment identifies what's driving the recurrence, treatment can address it rather than chase the symptom. Where and when a faulty stabilisation or movement pattern is the driver, the aim is to retrain it — because, as the recurrent-pain research above showed, these patterns can be changed with the right, specific practice.

This is where Dynamic Neuromuscular Stabilization (DNS) fits. DNS is an active approach built on developmental kinesiology — the way our nervous system first learns to stabilise and move as infants (Kobesová & Kolář, Journal of Bodywork and Movement Therapies, 2014). It retrains the deep stabilising system to work automatically again.

DNS exercises look simple, but the difference is in the quality of the movement, and it is subtle. Without someone watching and correcting in real time, it's easy to perform the exercise while the nervous system still uses the old compensatory pattern. In a one-on-one session, Eva can detect that and help you find the correct activation. The exercises start slow and simple — the goal is to establish correct stabilisation before adding complexity — and typically 10–20 minutes of daily practice is needed until the pattern becomes automatic.

It often starts with breathing

A common and easily missed factor in poor stability is a dysfunctional breathing pattern. The diaphragm isn't only for breathing — it also plays an important role in stabilising the trunk. When it contracts properly, it works with the pelvic floor and abdominal wall to create intra-abdominal pressure that supports the spine from the inside. When that coordination is off, the spine loses a layer of support it's supposed to have with every breath — which is why assessment and retraining so often start here.

Frequently asked questions

Should I see a chiropractor, physiotherapist, or massage therapist for pain that keeps coming back? Any of them can help — they're different tools, and the right one depends on your situation. What matters more than the profession is whether the underlying cause is assessed and addressed, rather than only the painful area. Recurring pain in particular tends to need a look at how you move and stabilise as a whole.

My physiotherapy isn't helping my sciatica — what should I do? Sciatica pain is often driven by patterns in the back, pelvis, and hips rather than only where the pain is felt. If treatment hasn't reached lasting relief, it's worth having the whole movement and stabilisation pattern assessed to check what's loading the nerve upstream, rather than continuing to treat the symptom alone.

Does "the pain has become more sensitive" mean it's in my head? No. Central sensitization is a real, physical change in how the nervous system processes signals (Woolf, Pain, 2011). The pain is genuine. Understanding this simply helps explain why pain can persist after an injury has healed — and why retraining and desensitising the system, not just treating tissue, can matter.

How long until the pain stops coming back? It depends on what the assessment finds and how long the pattern has been established. Some issues settle quickly; long-standing patterns take consistent retraining, often with short daily practice. Eva will give you a realistic picture after the assessment.

Can't I just do DNS exercises from a video? The exercises look simple, but their value depends on doing them with the correct activation — and it's easy to reproduce the old compensatory pattern without realising. Real-time correction from a practitioner is what makes the difference, at least until the pattern is established.
